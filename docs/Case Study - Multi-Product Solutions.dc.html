Multi-Product Solutions on AWS Marketplace

AWS Marketplace was built for listing one product at a time. I led design on the seller side of multi-product solutions: the Partner Central tools partners use to package software and services into one solution, take it from draft to a public listing, and sell it through a single offer set.

ROLE Lead Senior UX Designer
TEAM PM, Software Development Manager, multiple engineering teams, GTM
TIMELINE Jan – Nov 2025
LAUNCHED AWS re:Invent 2025; featured in CEO keynote
01 — The problem

Partners sell solutions. Marketplace only let them list products.

ISVs, system integrators, channel partners, and distributors already sell outcomes offline: their own software, other vendors' products they resell, and the services to tie it all together, pitched as one answer to a problem like zero-trust security or getting data ready for AI.

In Marketplace, that package had to be broken apart into separate listings and separate private offers, with nothing tying them together. Partners lost their story, and buyers were left to rebuild the bundle themselves and approve every purchase on its own. Partners had been asking for a way to sell solutions in Marketplace for years, and this project finally delivered it.

02 — Who it serves

Multiple sellers behind the deal, one point of contact for the buyer

Every solution has a lead seller who owns it and the relationship with the buyer. The lead brings in authorized resellers and partners whose software and services are part of the bundle. They collaborate behind the scenes on products, pricing, and delivery, so the buyer deals with one seller instead of coordinating several vendors. The buyer subscribes in one click and still gets a separate contract to manage for each product.

03 — Mapping the model Discover · Define

One solution to discover, a unique offer set for every buyer

Before designing any screens, I mapped how a multi-product solution should behave end to end with PM and engineering. The key decision was to split it into two entities: a public solution that every buyer discovers, and a private offer set created for each buyer who wants to purchase, with its own mix of products and terms.

05 — Process

Designed side by side with partners

Multi-product solutions is a new way for partners to sell, so I wanted the people who would use it involved from the start. I tested designs with ISVs, system integrators, channel partners, and distributors at every stage, from early concepts to near-final flows.

Focus groups
Hours of group sessions
I walked groups of partners through the creation and offer set flows to find where their real-world bundles didn't fit the model.
Partner 1:1s
Deep dives by seller type
One-on-one sessions with individual partners showed how an ISV, an SI, and a distributor each package and price a solution differently.
EMEA · APJ
Partner summits abroad
I traveled to AWS partner conferences and summits in EMEA and APJ to present the designs and get feedback from partners who wanted this feature.
Naming the feature

Research changed the product's name

The feature started as "composable solutions." Partners kept stumbling over the term: it described how the feature was built, not what they were selling. I ran surveys and spoke with partners directly to test terminology across the whole feature, wrote the proposal doc to rename it, and saw it through legal review. It launched as multi-product solutions.

Composable solutions → Multi-product solutions
What partners told us

"We sell other vendors' products every day." Resellers needed to add listings they don't own, which led to the "owned by someone else" option when adding products.

"Every component is negotiated separately." Partners wanted their own pricing and terms for each product, so the offer set keeps terms per component instead of one bundle price.

06 — Outcome
{{ s.v }} {{ s.l }}

Launch partners included Okta, Accenture, SCC, Westcon-Comstor, and TEKsystems, covering all four seller types from day one. In the months after launch, more partners published multi-product solutions, and buyers could buy bundled software and services in a single transaction instead of several separate ones.

07 — Reflection

Adding a new selling model to a mature platform isn't only a UI problem. The data model shapes every screen built on top of it, so I worked closely with engineering to define the API model for the solution entity, making sure its structure matched how buyers and partners actually think about solutions. Getting that foundation right early made every later design decision easier.
